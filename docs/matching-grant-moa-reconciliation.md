## 5. Your questions, answered

### 5.1 Can we drop the protein-coding markers (benA, CaM)? — Yes

Drop them. Three reasons it is the right call here: the MoA specifies ITS only, VOT 29000 is

**The condition: stop promising species-level identification of *Aspergillus* and

Concretely, change the language:

- "identify toxigenic species" → "identify taxa reported in the literature as toxigenic or
  biodeteriogenic", or "**putatively** toxigenic taxa"
- species-level claims → "genus-level assignment, with species-level assignment reported only

### 5.2 Metabarcoding plus culturing — how, and the funding problem

**First, a correction to my earlier advice.** I recommended culture-independent ITS
metabarcoding as the novel contribution. With the guidelines and quotes in hand, that
recommendation does not survive: **VOT 29000 is capped at RM3,000 and metabarcoding is
RM333 per sample** (NextGene META-ITS, Illumina NovaSeq PE250, 100,000 tags). That is **nine
samples**, before permit fees. Nine samples cannot support community analysis across
buildings, orientations and two monsoons. The signed MoA does not fund it either — TROPI's
Part 2 is RM17,136 of *reagents*, not sequencing services.

So there are two honest routes.

**Route A — Sanger-only, fully funded today, MoA-compliant.** This is what your partner is
actually set up to do: TROPI does isolation, sub-culturing, purification, DNA extraction and
PCR; you send amplicons for Sanger. At **RM25 per reaction (QT2610/0124)**, RM2,500 buys
**100 reactions** — roughly 50 isolates sequenced bidirectionally, or 100 single-pass. For a
first survey of a campus facade mycobiota that is a respectable, publishable dataset, and it
needs no variation and no extra money.

Its limitation is exactly what your §5.4 says: you characterise what grows on SDA and PDA.
**State that as a limitation rather than pretending otherwise**, and the internal
contradiction in your draft disappears.

**Route B — ask TROPI to redirect part of its own budget.** TROPI's Schedule B Part 2 line 2
is **RM17,136 for extraction and PCR consumables**, which is a very large reagent budget for
the isolate numbers implied. If TROPI agreed to move, say, RM10,000 of it to META-ITS, that
buys **30 metabarcoding samples** from their own funds — a far higher-value use of the same
ringgit, and it makes the project genuinely novel. It needs their agreement and a Clause 4.5
written variation.

**Recommendation: propose Route A in the submission, and open the Route B conversation with
Dr Sharon Lau in parallel.** Route A is fundable and defensible now; Route B is an upgrade you
can fold into the same addendum if she agrees. Do not submit a proposal whose method depends on
money nobody has budgeted.

**If Route B happens, the workflow is:** at each sampling point take the swab, then split it —
one half into a sterile tube for **direct DNA extraction** (the **FastDNA SPIN Kit for Soil**
in QT2610/0123, RM2,230 for 50 preps, is a reasonable bead-beating choice for this sample
type), the other half streaked onto SDA/PDA for culturing. Direct-extraction DNA goes to ITS
amplicon sequencing; cultured isolates go to Sanger. You then report the two side by side,
which is precisely the "complementary molecular and culture-based" approach your §5.4
advocates and your **ref 12 (Warren et al. 2026)** supports. To keep sample counts down,
**composite replicates**: pool the three replicate swabs from one surface-orientation-round
into one sequencing sample. Thirty sequencing samples can then represent ninety field swabs.

### 5.3 Contamination controls — concretely how

Facade swabs are low-biomass. At low biomass, DNA from the extraction kit, the reagents and
the air in the lab can make up a large share of your reads, and a reviewer in this area will
ask what you did about it. Four control types, all cheap:

1. **Field blank** — at each sampling round, open a sterile swab at the site, wave it in the
   air for the same time a real swab takes, **do not touch any surface**, and put it through
   the identical pipeline. Catches airborne and handling contamination. One per round.
2. **Transport blank** — a sterile unopened swab that travels in the same cooler to TROPI and
   back. Catches contamination during the cold chain. One per shipment.
3. **Extraction blank** — run the FastDNA kit with **no sample added**, just buffers. Catches
   kit and reagent contamination, which is the big one. **One per extraction batch**, and
   record which batch every real sample belongs to.
4. **PCR no-template control** — water in place of DNA in the PCR. One per PCR plate. Also run
   a **positive control**: a known fungal isolate or a mock community, so a blank result is
   distinguishable from a failed reaction.

Then three rules that matter as much as the controls themselves:

- **Carry the blanks all the way through.** Do not stop at "the blank was clean on the gel" —
  a gel will not show low-level contamination. If you are sequencing, **sequence the blanks
  too**, with the same library prep. Budget for them: three blanks under Route B is three
  more RM333 samples, so plan them in rather than discovering them later.
- **Report them.** One short paragraph in the methods and a line in the results stating what
  the blanks contained. This is what converts a potential criticism into evidence of rigour.
- **Remove contaminant signal statistically**, not by eye. The standard tool is the R package
  **decontam**, which uses either prevalence in blanks or the relationship with DNA
  concentration to flag contaminant sequence variants. Name it in the methodology.

For the culture side the equivalents are: incubate **uninoculated media plates** from each
batch to confirm sterility, and expose an **open plate** in the lab during sample handling to
quantify laboratory fallout. Both cost a few plates.

Two sentences in Phase 2 and Phase 3 cover all of this. Its absence is a bigger problem than
its cost.

### 5.4 The mortar-coupon assay — what it is for, and why to drop it

**Purpose.** Taxonomy tells you *who is there*. It does not tell you that those organisms are
damaging your buildings. The coupon assay is the bridge: you take small cast mortar or cement
specimens, inoculate them with isolates you recovered, keep uninoculated specimens as
controls, incubate for weeks to months, and then measure what changed — **mass loss** after
cleaning and drying, **surface pH shift** from acid secretion, and **oxalate or acid
production** in the medium. If inoculated coupons lose mass and controls do not, you have
demonstrated biodeterioration by organisms from your own facades rather than asserting it from
the literature.

**But you asked whether you can skip it, and the answer is yes — you should.** Reasons:

- It is a months-long incubation that does not fit comfortably inside 24 months alongside two
  monsoon campaigns.
- It is not in the MoA, so adding it is scope expansion at exactly the moment you want scope
  discipline.
- Your budget has no room for it once sequencing is funded.
- And you do not need it, provided you stop claiming to identify "material-degrading species".

**Instead, reframe.** Identify the taxa present, then cite the existing literature for their
known biodeteriogenic activity. You already hold strong references for exactly this: **ref 16
(Chaudhuri et al., in-vitro deterioration of concrete and marble by *Aspergillus tamarii*,
J. Building Engineering 2020)**, **ref 17 (Fomina et al., fungal deterioration of barrier
concrete, Geomicrobiology Journal 2007)**, **ref 18 (Jiang et al., critical review of microbial
degradation of concrete by fungi, Building and Environment 2022)** and **ref 19 (Gaylarde &
Morton on biofilms in deterioration of constructional materials)**. That lets you write
"*taxa recovered include genera reported to degrade cementitious materials [16,17,18]*", which
is defensible and honest, instead of "we identified material-degrading species", which needs
an assay you have not run.

Keep the coupon assay as the headline of the **follow-on** proposal. It is a natural second
project, it is your actual expertise, and having the isolate collection from this grant is what
makes it possible. Say so in the Expected Result field — a credible next step strengthens an
application.

### 5.5 "Can we just design it around identifying the microbes on UTS building exteriors?" — Yes, and it is a better proposal

This is the strongest instinct you have had in this conversation, and I would act on it.

A **descriptive community survey** — what colonises the exteriors of a tropical campus, how it
varies with material, orientation, exposure and season, with air quality and microclimate
recorded as context — is coherent, achievable at RM25,000 in 24 months, needs no causal
identification, and is publishable on its own terms. It also matches what you can actually
fund. The current draft's problem is that it promises a causal API–fungal relationship that
neither the budget nor the design can deliver.

**The green-building angle is genuinely good and you should use it.** A green-certified campus
showing visible mould and algae is an interesting tension, not an embarrassment: several green
design strategies — deep shading, reduced solar exposure on facades, natural ventilation,
vegetation close to the envelope, water features, high-albedo or porous finishes — plausibly
*increase* surface moisture persistence and therefore biofouling. "Does green-building design
trade energy performance for biodeterioration risk in the humid tropics?" is a sharper research
question than anything currently in the draft, it is policy-relevant for JKR and campus
operators exactly as Schedule A A6 promises, and I am not aware of it being well studied in
equatorial Borneo. Your facade and passive-design references support the framing: **refs 24,
28, 33, 38 and 40**.

**Include the algae.** You see algae; your §5.1 excludes them on the grounds that they are only
aesthetic. If the question is now "what is growing here", that exclusion stops making sense —
and on tropical facades algae and cyanobacteria are typically the pioneer colonisers that hold
moisture and let fungi establish, so excluding them weakens your paired "clean" controls.
Minimum: **score algal and cyanobacterial cover visually at every point** as a covariate, which
costs nothing. Note that the MoA's own Schedule A A1 already refers to "mould, algae and
fungi", so this is consistent with the signed scope.

If budget ever allows, the **16S quote (META-16S, 48 samples, RM15,984)** would capture
cyanobacteria alongside bacteria and make it a true microbial-community study — but that is
Route B territory or a follow-on grant, not this RM25,000.

### 5.6 Should you pick different buildings? — Yes, and this is the cheapest upgrade available

Sampling one building gives you a case study. Sampling several gives you a survey with
contrasts, which is what makes a paper.

**Stratify deliberately.** Choose 3–4 buildings that differ on axes you can defend:

- **Facade material** — concrete, painted render, tile. Schedule A A3(c) already names these.
- **Age** — a newer and an older block; cumulative exposure differs.
- **Green-certified versus conventional**, if your campus has both. This is your novel axis.
- **Exposure and shading** — a shaded, sheltered facade versus an exposed one.

Then within each building keep what Schedule A A3(b) already requires: fixed points on north,
south, east and west walls plus roof surfaces, with paired colonised and visually clean points
of the same material.

That design answers four questions instead of one — does community composition differ by
material, by orientation, by building age or design, and by season — and none of it costs more
than sampling one building would, because the cost driver is the number of samples, not the
number of addresses. **Multiple buildings needs no MoA variation**: Schedule A says "building
exteriors at the University of Technology Sarawak, Sibu", not a single named block.

Do state the final sample count. Something like 4 buildings × 4 orientations × paired
colonised/clean × 3 rounds, composited to a stated number of sequencing samples, with
replicate structure spelled out.
