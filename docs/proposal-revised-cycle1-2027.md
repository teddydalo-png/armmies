## 8. Executive Summary

*(298 words — limit 300)*

Building exteriors in equatorial Sarawak are continuously exposed to high humidity, heavy
rainfall and fluctuating air quality, including recurrent regional haze. Mould, algae and fungi
are among the most common organic contaminants on these surfaces, degrading appearance and
material performance, yet exterior colonisation in the humid tropics is treated as a cleaning
problem rather than a measured phenomenon. Which organisms are present on tropical educational
building facades, and how they vary with material, orientation, exposure and season, is largely
undocumented for equatorial Borneo.

This 24-month collaborative project between UTS and SRDC-TROPI characterises the fungal and
associated microbial communities colonising building exteriors at the UTS Sibu campus, and
records campus air quality and microclimate as environmental context. UTS will establish fixed
sampling points across four buildings stratified by facade material, age, design approach and
exposure, with paired visibly colonised and visually clean surfaces as internal controls, and
will sample quarterly across a full annual seasonal round, with two monsoon phases
resampled twelve months apart. Plates inoculated at UTS are incubated, enumerated and isolated
at SRDC-TROPI, which performs DNA extraction and PCR; sequencing of selected isolates is
outsourced for identification against the internal transcribed spacer region.
Algal and cyanobacterial cover will be scored at every point as a covariate, recognising these
as pioneer colonisers that condition surfaces for fungal establishment. UTS will additionally
deploy low-cost air-quality and microclimate monitoring with a public campus dashboard.

Expected outputs are the first documented inventory of facade-colonising fungi for a tropical
Malaysian campus, two indexed joint publications, a curated isolate collection, exterior
material selection and maintenance guidance, a campus air-quality reporting protocol, and
trained student researchers. The project supports the Sarawak Post COVID-19 Development
Strategy 2030 under Environmental Sustainability by giving MEITD, JKR and campus operators
measured evidence on biodeterioration of tropical public buildings.

## 9. Background of Research

### 9.1 Problem statement

Educational buildings in the humid tropics carry visible biological colonisation on their
exteriors within a few years of completion. At the UTS Sibu campus — a modern, green-design
campus — mould, algal films and fungal staining are present across multiple building facades.
The institutional response is reactive chemical washing, which is recurrent, costly and
addresses appearance rather than cause.

Three gaps follow. First, **nobody knows what is growing there.** No inventory of
facade-colonising fungi exists for a Malaysian campus of this type, and to our knowledge none
exists for equatorial Borneo; the available tropical building-mycology literature is dominated
by indoor environments [7,32,48] and by temperate or subtropical settings [1,2,44,47]. Second,
**the drivers are unmeasured locally.** Moisture availability is established as the primary
control on fungal colonisation of building materials [4,31,34,39], and facade orientation,
shading and ventilation are known to modulate surface moisture retention [24,28,38,40], but
these relationships have not been characterised for equatorial, green-designed educational
buildings. Third, **maintenance decisions have no evidence base.** Without knowing which taxa
are present and where they concentrate, facility managers cannot select resistant finishes or
schedule intervention by risk rather than by appearance.

A specific tension motivates the work. Green building design in the humid tropics favours deep
shading, reduced facade insolation, natural ventilation, planting close to the envelope and
porous or textured finishes. Each of these plausibly extends the duration of surface wetness,
which is the condition fungal colonisation requires [4,31,39]. Whether green design
inadvertently raises biodeterioration risk in equatorial climates is, as far as we can
establish, untested — and it is directly relevant to how public buildings in Sarawak are
specified.

### 9.2 Research questions

1. Which fungal taxa colonise the exteriors of tropical educational buildings at UTS Sibu, and
   what is their relative abundance?
2. Does community composition and abundance differ by facade material, building age, design
   approach and orientation?
3. Does composition vary between monsoon phases, and is that seasonal signal reproducible
   across two consecutive cycles?
4. Is algal and cyanobacterial cover associated with fungal abundance, consistent with pioneer
   facilitation?
5. (Exploratory) Is fungal abundance associated with antecedent particulate matter and
   microclimate after adjustment for humidity and rainfall?

### 9.3 Hypotheses

- **H1.** Fungal abundance and diversity differ significantly between facade orientations, with
  shaded, low-insolation facades supporting higher values, consistent with longer surface
  wetness duration [4,31,39].
- **H2.** Community composition differs between facade materials (concrete, painted render,
  tile), reflecting substrate moisture adsorption and nutrient availability [4,32].
- **H3.** Abundance and composition differ between Northeast and Southwest monsoon phases, and
  the direction of that difference is consistent when two seasonal phases are resampled twelve
  months apart [36,37].
- **H4.** Surfaces with higher algal and cyanobacterial cover support higher fungal abundance,
  consistent with biofilm pioneers conditioning the substrate [19].
- **H5 (exploratory).** Fungal abundance is positively associated with antecedent particulate
  matter concentration after adjustment for relative humidity and rainfall [5].

### 9.4 Literature review

**Moisture is the primary control.** Laboratory and modelling work establishes relative humidity
and surface moisture as the dominant determinants of fungal colonisation on building materials.
Humidity level governs biodeterioration rate on mineral substrates [1]; the moisture adsorption
properties of the material itself condition growth [4]; hyphal extension rate is a direct
function of humidity [31]; and moisture parameters structure the fungal communities that
establish on building surfaces [34]. The relationship between moisture, ventilation and mould is
long established in the conservation literature [39].

**Building design modulates that moisture.** Facade geometry, glazing ratio, shading and
ventilation strategy determine how long exterior surfaces remain wet. Work on tropical
educational buildings addresses the daylight and energy optimisation of facades [24,40] and the
retrofit of passive design measures [28,33], while studies in hot and humid climates address
mould prevention under high ventilation rates [38]. Occupant-driven variation in ventilation
openings adds further variability [25]. These literatures optimise for energy and comfort;
biodeterioration risk is not generally among their objective functions, which is the gap this
project addresses.

**Dominant taxa on built surfaces.** *Penicillium* is consistently recovered from school
buildings [44], *Cladosporium sphaerospermum* predominates on indoor surfaces owing to
xerotolerance, osmoregulation and melanisation that allow persistence under fluctuating water
activity [9], and *Aspergillus* lineages are specifically associated with the built environment
[11]. Emerging tropical and subtropical taxa continue to be described [8]. In Malaysia, fungal
assessment of older buildings in the Peninsula [48] and the detection of fungal DNA and
mycotoxins in Johor Bahru classroom dust, with associated occupant symptoms [7], establish both
local relevance and a health dimension. Comparable exterior inventories for Borneo are absent.

**Biodeterioration mechanisms.** Fungal colonisation damages mineral substrates biochemically
and biomechanically. A critical review of microbial degradation of concrete by fungi
synthesises the physiological, ecological and chemical pathways [18]; *Aspergillus tamarii* has
been shown in vitro to deteriorate concrete and marble [16]; fungal deterioration of barrier
concrete is documented in waste-containment contexts [17]; and marble substrates show
mycologically mediated decay in heritage settings [47]. Biofilm formation and extracellular
polymeric substance production accelerate deterioration and surface soiling [19], and
colonisation on building materials responds to environmental stressors in ways still being
characterised [20]. Comparative work on traditional versus modern construction in coastal
tropical India shows material-specific susceptibility [32].

**Air quality as context.** Atmospheric particulate matter and fungal spore loads are coupled:
PM10 concentration co-varies with atmospheric fungal spore counts under tropical urban
conditions [5], regional-scale spore concentrations can be modelled from atmospheric data [41],
and integrated particulate sensing is being applied to bioaerosol dynamics [27]. Occupancy
itself elevates measured fungal and bacterial loads in classrooms [6], and school environments
show systematic indoor-outdoor and seasonal structure in airborne fungi [3,29,36,37,43]. This
project therefore records air quality and microclimate as environmental context for the surface
communities, not as an experimentally manipulated exposure.

**Detection approach.** Culture-based and molecular methods are complementary rather than
interchangeable: culture recovers viable organisms but underestimates non-culturable and
stressed taxa, while molecular detection is more sensitive and faster [12]. Swab type materially
affects recovery efficiency [14], multiplex real-time PCR assays are available for filamentous
fungi [13], and fungal volatile organic compounds offer an orthogonal signal [15]. The ITS
region is the accepted primary fungal barcode [26], though classification of ITS amplicons
depends on the assignment method used [35] and resolution is limited for some genera. Mitigation
options — antifungal nanocoatings [21,22] and non-thermal plasma [23] — define the intervention
space that an inventory of locally present taxa would inform.

### 9.5 Relevance to government policy, community and industry

The project aligns with the **Sarawak Post COVID-19 Development Strategy 2030** under
Environmental Sustainability and Social Well-being [PCDS 2030]. It gives MEITD, JKR and public
campus operators measured evidence on the biological deterioration of tropical public building
exteriors, supporting specification and maintenance decisions currently made without local data.
Outputs transfer directly to schools, clinics and offices across Sarawak, which share the
climate, the construction types and the reactive-washing maintenance pattern. The isolate
collection and air-quality baseline remain as institutional assets, and the campus monitoring
capability supports UTS environmental reporting.

## 11. Methodology

### Phase 1 — Site stratification and setup (Mar – May 2027)

**Building selection.** Four buildings on the UTS Sibu campus, stratified to generate contrast
on four axes rather than to describe one structure: **facade material** (exposed concrete,
painted render, tile), **age** (newer and older blocks, differing in cumulative exposure),
**design approach** (green-design features — deep shading, planting near the envelope, natural
ventilation — versus conventional), and **exposure** (sheltered versus wind- and rain-exposed
elevations). Facade material inventory, orientation and shading geometry are mapped for each.

**Sampling points.** At each building, fixed points on north, south, east and west-facing walls
plus one roof-adjacent surface. At each wall point, a **paired** pair of locations is
established: one visibly colonised and one visually clean surface **of the same material**, as
an internal control. This gives 4 buildings × 4 orientations × 2 (colonised/clean) = **32 wall
points**, plus 4 roof points = **36 fixed points**, georeferenced and photographed for repeat
sampling.

**Air quality and microclimate.** Portable monitoring nodes are fabricated on a microcontroller
platform in ventilated IP65 weather-resistant enclosures, measuring PM2.5, PM10, CO, NOx, O₃,
CO₂, temperature and relative humidity. The configuration follows one **already validated in
deployed service** by the co-researcher under existing work — a multi-parameter air-quality
module for particulates, carbon dioxide, temperature and humidity, a discrete temperature and
humidity sensor as a redundant reference, and an MQ-series gas sensor set — so the expenditure
replicates a proven build rather than carrying development risk. Nodes transmit to a cloud
database over campus Wi-Fi or LoRa, with a public dashboard and a display unit at a high-traffic
campus location. Devices are co-located with a rented reference-grade instrument
for a calibration campaign under Sibu conditions before deployment.

Two measurement limitations are stated explicitly rather than left for a reviewer to find.
First, **the Malaysian Air Pollution Index is a Department of Environment index incorporating an
SO₂ sub-index, which this instrumentation does not measure.** Sulphur dioxide is not a
determinant of surface fungal colonisation in any mechanism this project tests, so it is not
instrumented and no SO₂ sensing is budgeted. Values reported here are therefore an *indicative
campus air-quality index* computed from the available sub-indices, and are compared against the
nearest DOE monitoring station for the regional signal rather than presented as official API. Second, **low-cost optical particulate sensors over-report mass at
high relative humidity** because hygroscopic particle growth increases light scattering — the
regime in which this campus routinely sits. A published hygroscopic growth correction is applied
and **both corrected and uncorrected values are reported**, and relative humidity is retained as
a covariate in every model involving particulate matter. Any particulate–fungal association
(H5) is reported as a partial association after that adjustment, and treated as exploratory.

**Protocol validation.** Culture media, swab handling and the full contamination-control set are
validated on a pilot round before the first formal sampling round.

### Phase 2 — Sampling and plating (Apr 2027 – Jul 2028)

**Surface sampling.** At each point, a **10 × 10 cm sterile template** defines the sampled area.
Three replicate sterile swabs are taken per point per round, moistened in sterile transport
medium. Swab type is held constant throughout, recognising that recovery efficiency is
swab-dependent [14]. Passive settle plates are deployed adjacent to a defined subset of points
for airborne spore capture.

**Covariates recorded at every point, every round.** Algal and cyanobacterial cover as
percentage cover on a fixed ordinal scale; surface moisture presence; substrate material;
orientation; shading index; and the preceding rainfall, temperature, humidity and particulate
record from the monitoring network.

**Sampling rounds.** Six quarterly rounds between April 2027 and July 2028, with up to two
additional rounds triggered opportunistically during regional haze episodes:

| Round | Month | Seasonal phase |
| --- | --- | --- |
| R1 | Apr 2027 | Inter-monsoon, onset of Southwest |
| R2 | Jul 2027 | Southwest monsoon |
| R3 | Oct 2027 | Inter-monsoon, pre-Northeast |
| R4 | Jan 2028 | Northeast monsoon |
| R5 | Apr 2028 | Inter-monsoon — **twelve-month repeat of R1** |
| R6 | Jul 2028 | Southwest monsoon — **twelve-month repeat of R2** |

R1 to R4 constitute a **complete annual seasonal round**, covering both monsoon phases and both
inter-monsoon periods. R5 and R6 resample the R1 and R2 phases exactly twelve months later,
giving paired year-on-year observations for two of the four phases.

This is the maximum seasonal structure obtainable inside a fixed 24-month award. The project
must close on 28 February 2029 with no extension, so sampling ends in July 2028 to leave seven
months for the remaining molecular work, analysis, both manuscripts and grant closure. **Full
inter-annual replication across both monsoon phases is explicitly outside the scope of this
award** and is identified as follow-on work.

At 36 points per round, the three replicate swabs are **composited per point per round**, giving
**36 samples per round and 216 composited samples** over six rounds.

**Plating and transfer.** Under the agreed division of labour, SRDC-TROPI supplies prepared
sterile swabs and media to UTS ahead of each round. UTS performs the swabbing and inoculates SDA
and PDA plates on site, then returns the inoculated plates and swabs to SRDC-TROPI under
cold-chain conditions for incubation. This keeps media preparation and incubation inside the
partner's controlled laboratory environment while field sampling stays with the UTS team, and it
avoids duplicate procurement of the same consumables across the two budgets.

**Contamination controls.** Low-biomass surface sampling is vulnerable to reagent, kit and
airborne contamination, so the following are carried through the identical pipeline:

- **Field blank** — one per round. A sterile swab is opened at the sampling site, exposed to air
  for the duration of a normal swab, **contacts no surface**, and is processed identically.
- **Transport blank** — one per shipment. A sterile unopened swab travels in the cooler to
  SRDC-TROPI and back.
- **Extraction blank** — **one per extraction batch**. The extraction kit is run with buffers
  only and no sample input, capturing kit and reagent background. Every sample's batch
  membership is recorded.
- **PCR no-template control** — one per PCR plate, water in place of template — together with a
  **positive control** of a known isolate, so that a negative blank is distinguishable from a
  failed reaction.
- **Culture-side controls** — uninoculated media plates from every batch incubated to confirm
  sterility, and an open plate exposed during sample handling to quantify laboratory fallout.

Blanks are **carried through the entire pipeline and sequenced alongside samples**, not screened
out at the gel stage, since gel electrophoresis does not resolve low-level contamination. The
content of all blanks is **reported in the results**. Where sequence data permits, contaminant
signal is removed statistically using prevalence-based decontamination against the blank set
rather than by inspection.

### Phase 3 — Incubation, isolation and identification (rolling, Apr 2027 – Oct 2028)

Inoculated plates and swabs are transported to SRDC-TROPI in Kota Samarahan in sealed,
triple-packaged containers under cold-chain conditions. SRDC-TROPI incubates all plates and
enumerates growth, with colony counts expressed as **colony-forming units per unit area** for
surfaces and per cubic metre for settle plates. This quantitative dataset across all 216
composited samples is the statistical backbone of H1 to H4. Colonies are separated by morphotype
and purified, and a curated set of up to **100 unique isolates** spanning the morphotype range,
buildings, materials and seasons is selected for identification. SRDC-TROPI performs DNA
extraction and PCR amplification of the **internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region** using
fungal-specific primers, and forwards the amplicons to an accredited commercial provider for
Sanger sequencing, funded from the UTS Matching Grant under VOT 29000. Isolates are maintained
by SRDC-TROPI as a referenced collection.
Sequences are quality-trimmed, assembled and assigned taxonomically against a curated fungal ITS
reference database, with the assignment method and confidence threshold stated, recognising that
ITS amplicon classification is method-dependent [35].

**Processing is rolling, not batched.** Each sampling round is transported and processed as it
completes, so that first-cycle samples are identified while second-cycle sampling continues.
This yields an interim community profile at twelve months, permits protocol correction before
two years of samples accumulate, and de-risks the schedule.

**Stated limitations of the identification approach.** Two are acknowledged in the design rather
than the discussion. First, this is a **culture-dependent** characterisation: culture recovers
viable organisms but systematically underestimates non-culturable and stressed environmental
taxa [12], so the inventory describes the culturable fraction and is reported as such.
Culture-independent ITS amplicon profiling is identified as the priority extension and is costed
as an option at §12. Second, **ITS is the accepted primary fungal barcode [26] but does not
reliably resolve all genera to species level.** Assignments are therefore reported at genus
level, with species-level identification given only where ITS support is unambiguous. Taxa are
described as **putatively toxigenic or biodeteriogenic** on the basis of published activity for
the genus [16,17,18,19], not on the basis of assay in this project. Secondary protein-coding
markers for finer resolution are identified as follow-on work.

### Phase 4 — Analysis, synthesis and closure (Aug 2028 – Feb 2029)

**Statistical analysis.** Colony-forming-unit abundance is modelled with generalised linear
mixed models appropriate to count data, with building, orientation, facade material, design
approach, season and algal cover as fixed effects and sampling point as a random effect to
respect repeated measures. Community composition is analysed by permutational multivariate
analysis of variance on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities with ordination for visualisation, and
alpha diversity by standard indices. Paired colonised and clean surfaces are compared within
point pairs. The exploratory particulate association (H5) is fitted with antecedent-window
particulate means and humidity, rainfall and temperature as adjustment covariates; it is
reported as exploratory and multiplicity is acknowledged.

**Synthesis.** Findings are translated into exterior material selection and maintenance guidance
— which materials and orientations warrant intervention, and on what interval — and into a
campus air-quality reporting protocol supporting institutional environmental reporting.

### Regulatory compliance, biosafety and data

**Permits.** Research in Sarawak requires a permit issued by the Sarawak Biodiversity Council
through SORAS. UTS holds **SBC Permit No. SBC-2026-RDP-10-AIO**, issued 28 July 2026 and valid
to 27 July 2027. A **variation covering the Project title, scope, personnel and institutions**
and a **renewal** are submitted through SORAS during the preparatory period before the project
commences, so that sampling is not gated by permit processing after the start date. Permit
status, including the period granted on renewal, is tracked as a named milestone with the
Principal Researcher as owner. Deposition of sequence data and isolates is undertaken in
accordance with the permit conditions.

**Biosafety.** Culturing concentrates fungal genera that include potentially pathogenic and
toxigenic species. Work is conducted at the containment level specified by the UTS biosafety
assessment, with a documented risk assessment, defined personal protective equipment,
biological safety cabinet handling of cultures, autoclave waste disposal, and clearance for
undergraduate and postgraduate participation. Transport of biological material between
institutions follows the triple-packaging and cold-chain requirements of MoA Clause 3.3(b).

**Data.** Raw air-quality records, colony counts, covariate records and sequence data are shared
between the parties for joint publication and reporting under MoA Clause 3.3(c). Sequences are
deposited in a public nucleotide database with accessions reported, subject to permit
conditions. The air-quality dataset is released as an open campus dataset.

**Limitations.** The design is observational and establishes association, not causation. It
characterises the culturable fraction of the community. Seasonal structure is assessed over one
complete annual round with two phases resampled twelve months apart, so year-on-year
consistency can be tested for those two phases only; full inter-annual replication across all
four phases is not achievable within a fixed 24-month term and is identified as follow-on work. Sampling is confined to one campus, so
generalisation to other Sarawak building stock is indicative; the stratified building selection
is intended to maximise the transferable contrast available within that constraint.

### Three claims from the original draft that were cut

Each lacked support in the Scopus list, and each was a liability rather than a strength:

1. **"Fungal spores contribute up to 20 percent of particulate matter mass in warmer months."**
   Cut. No reference in the list clearly reports this figure. If you locate the source, it can be
   reinstated at §9.4 under "Air quality as context". A specific percentage with no citation is
   the first thing a careful reviewer checks.
2. **"Reduced ultraviolet radiation during haze episodes may favour fungal survival by mitigating
   photoinhibition."** Cut. Nothing in the list supports it and it was the most speculative
   sentence in the draft.
3. **"Algae primarily cause aesthetic discolouration while fungi penetrate materials."** Cut as a
   scope justification. Replaced by including algal and cyanobacterial cover as a measured
   covariate (Objective 4, H4), justified on pioneer facilitation [19]. This converts the
   weakest paragraph in the draft into a research question.

concrete, painted render and tile, placing timber outside the project's own scope.
