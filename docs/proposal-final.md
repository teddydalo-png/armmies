## 8. Executive summary

Building exteriors in equatorial Sarawak are continuously exposed to high humidity, heavy
rainfall and fluctuating air quality, including recurrent regional haze. Mould, algae and fungi
are among the most common organic contaminants on these surfaces, degrading both appearance and
material performance, yet exterior colonisation in the humid tropics is managed as a cleaning
problem rather than measured as a phenomenon. Which organisms colonise tropical educational
building facades, and how they vary with material, orientation, exposure and season, is largely
undocumented for equatorial Borneo.

This twenty-four month collaborative project between UTS and SRDC-TROPI characterises the fungal
and associated microbial communities colonising building exteriors at the UTS Sibu campus, and
records campus air quality and microclimate as environmental context. UTS will establish fixed
sampling points across four buildings stratified by facade material, age, design approach and
exposure, with paired visibly colonised and visually clean surfaces serving as internal
controls, and will sample quarterly across a full annual seasonal round with two phases
resampled twelve months apart. Plates inoculated at UTS are incubated, enumerated and isolated
at SRDC-TROPI, which performs DNA extraction and PCR; sequencing of selected isolates is
outsourced for identification against the internal transcribed spacer region. Algal and
cyanobacterial cover is scored at every point as a covariate, recognising these organisms as
pioneer colonisers that condition surfaces for fungal establishment.

Expected outputs are the first documented inventory of facade-colonising fungi for a tropical
Malaysian campus, two indexed joint publications, a curated isolate collection, exterior
material selection and maintenance guidance, a campus air-quality reporting protocol, and
trained student researchers. The project supports the Sarawak Post COVID-19 Development Strategy
2030 under Environmental Sustainability by providing MEITD, JKR and campus operators with
measured evidence on the biodeterioration of tropical public buildings [44].

## 9. Background of research

### 9.1 Problem statement

Educational buildings in the humid tropics acquire visible biological colonisation on their
exteriors within a few years of completion. At the UTS Sibu campus, which is of modern green
design, mould, algal films and fungal staining are present across multiple building facades. The
institutional response is reactive chemical washing, which recurs, consumes maintenance budget
and addresses appearance rather than cause.

Three gaps follow from this. The first is that the organisms responsible have not been
identified. No inventory of facade-colonising fungi exists for a Malaysian campus of this type,
and the available Malaysian and tropical literature concerns indoor environments [1, 2, 3],
while exterior studies are drawn largely from temperate and subtropical settings [4, 5, 6, 7].
The second is that the local drivers are unmeasured. Moisture availability is well established
as the primary control on fungal colonisation of building materials [8, 9, 10, 11], and facade
orientation, shading and ventilation are known to modulate how long exterior surfaces remain
wet [12, 13, 14, 15], but these relationships have not been characterised for equatorial,
green-designed educational buildings. The third is that maintenance decisions therefore have no
local evidence base. Without knowing which taxa are present, and where on a building they
concentrate, facility managers cannot select resistant finishes or schedule intervention by risk
rather than by appearance.

A specific tension motivates the work. Green building design in the humid tropics favours deep
shading, reduced facade insolation, natural ventilation, planting close to the envelope and
porous or textured finishes. Each of these plausibly extends the duration of surface wetness,
which is the condition that fungal colonisation requires [8, 9, 11]. Whether green design
inadvertently raises biodeterioration risk in equatorial climates appears to be untested, and it
bears directly on how public buildings in Sarawak are specified.

### 9.2 Hypotheses

H1. Fungal abundance and diversity differ significantly between facade orientations, with
shaded, low-insolation facades supporting higher values, consistent with longer surface wetness
duration [8, 9, 11].

H2. Community composition differs between facade materials, namely exposed concrete, painted
render and tile, reflecting differences in substrate moisture adsorption and nutrient
availability [8, 3].

H3. Abundance and composition differ between the Northeast and Southwest monsoon phases, and the
direction of that difference is consistent when two seasonal phases are resampled twelve months
apart [16, 17].

H4. Surfaces carrying higher algal and cyanobacterial cover support higher fungal abundance,
consistent with biofilm pioneers conditioning the substrate [18].

H5, exploratory. Fungal abundance is positively associated with antecedent particulate matter
concentration after adjustment for relative humidity and rainfall [19].

### 9.3 Research questions

1. Which fungal taxa colonise the exteriors of tropical educational buildings at UTS Sibu, and
   what is their relative abundance?
2. Does community composition and abundance differ by facade material, building age, design
   approach and orientation?
3. Does composition vary between monsoon phases, and is that seasonal signal consistent when a
   phase is resampled a year later?
4. Is algal and cyanobacterial cover associated with fungal abundance, as pioneer facilitation
   would predict?
5. Is fungal abundance associated with antecedent particulate matter and microclimate once
   humidity and rainfall are accounted for?

### 9.4 Literature review

Relative humidity and surface moisture are consistently identified as the dominant determinants
of fungal colonisation on building materials. Humidity level governs the rate of biodeterioration
on mineral substrates [4], the moisture adsorption properties of the material itself condition
growth [8], hyphal extension rate varies directly with humidity [9], and moisture parameters
structure the fungal communities that establish on building surfaces [10]. The relationship
between moisture, ventilation and mould growth has been recognised in the conservation
literature for several decades [11].

Building design governs how long that moisture persists. Facade geometry, glazing ratio, shading
and ventilation strategy together determine the duration of surface wetness. Studies of tropical
educational buildings have addressed the daylight and energy optimisation of facades [12, 15],
the retrofitting of passive design measures [13, 20] and the prevention of mould growth under
high mechanical ventilation rates in hot and humid climates [14]. Occupant-driven variation in
the operation of windows, doors and fans introduces further variability in naturally ventilated
school buildings [21]. These literatures optimise for energy performance and thermal comfort;
biodeterioration risk does not generally appear among their objectives, which is the gap this
project addresses.

The taxa recovered from built surfaces are reasonably consistent across studies. Penicillium is
routinely isolated from school buildings [6]. Cladosporium sphaerospermum predominates on indoor
surfaces, its xerotolerance supported by osmoregulation and melanisation that permit persistence
under fluctuating water activity [22]. Aspergillus lineages specifically associated with the
built environment continue to be described [23], as do emerging tropical and subtropical taxa
[24]. Within Malaysia, fungal assessment of older buildings in the Peninsula [1] and the
detection of fungal DNA and mycotoxins in classroom dust in Johor Bahru, with associated
occupant symptoms [2], establish both local relevance and a health dimension. Comparable
exterior inventories for Borneo are absent from the indexed literature.

The mechanisms by which colonisation damages mineral substrates are well characterised. A
critical review of the microbial degradation of concrete by fungi synthesises the physiological,
ecological, physical and chemical pathways involved [25]. Aspergillus tamarii has been shown in
vitro to deteriorate both concrete and marble [26], fungal deterioration of barrier concrete is
documented in waste containment contexts [27], and marble substrates show mycologically mediated
decay in heritage settings [7]. Biofilm formation and the production of extracellular polymeric
substances accelerate deterioration and surface soiling [18], colonisation responds to
environmental stressors in ways still being characterised [28], and comparative work on
traditional and modern construction in coastal tropical India demonstrates material-specific
susceptibility [3].

Air quality forms part of the environmental context in which these communities develop.
Particulate matter concentration co-varies with atmospheric fungal spore counts under tropical
urban conditions [19], regional spore concentrations can be modelled from atmospheric data [29],
and integrated particulate sensing is being applied to bioaerosol dynamics [30]. Occupancy
itself elevates measured fungal and bacterial loads in classrooms [31], and school environments
show systematic indoor to outdoor and seasonal structure in airborne fungi [32, 33, 16, 17, 34].
This project accordingly records air quality and microclimate as environmental context for the
surface communities rather than as an experimentally manipulated exposure.

Culture-based and molecular detection methods are complementary rather than interchangeable.
Culture recovers viable organisms but underestimates non-culturable and stressed taxa, whereas
molecular detection is faster and more sensitive [35]. Swab type materially affects recovery
efficiency [36], multiplex real-time PCR assays are available for filamentous fungi [37], and
fungal volatile organic compounds offer an orthogonal signal [38]. The internal transcribed
spacer region is the accepted primary fungal barcode [39], although the classification of ITS
amplicons depends on the assignment method adopted [40] and resolution is limited within certain
genera.

Finally, the intervention space is reasonably well defined. Antifungal nanocoatings based on
silver and zinc oxide [41, 42] and non-thermal plasma treatment [43] have both been evaluated.
An inventory of the taxa actually present on local facades is what would allow such options to
be assessed for this building stock, which is the practical contribution this project seeks to
make.

## 11. Methodology

### Phase 1. Site stratification and setup, March to May 2027

Four buildings on the UTS Sibu campus are selected to generate contrast rather than to describe
a single structure. The stratification axes are facade material, covering exposed concrete,
painted render and tile; building age, contrasting newer and older blocks that differ in
cumulative exposure; design approach, contrasting green design features such as deep shading,
planting close to the envelope and natural ventilation against conventional construction; and
exposure, contrasting sheltered elevations against wind-exposed and rain-exposed ones. Facade
material inventory, orientation and shading geometry are mapped for each building.

At each building, fixed points are established on north, south, east and west facing walls
together with one roof-adjacent surface. At each wall point a pair of locations is established,
one visibly colonised and one visually clean, on the same material, to serve as an internal
control. This yields thirty-two wall points, being four buildings by four orientations by two
surface conditions, together with four roof points, giving thirty-six fixed points in total.
All points are georeferenced and photographed to permit repeat sampling.

Portable monitoring nodes are fabricated on a microcontroller platform in ventilated,
weather-resistant enclosures rated to IP65, measuring PM2.5, PM10, carbon monoxide, oxides of
nitrogen, ozone, carbon dioxide, temperature and relative humidity. The configuration follows one
already validated in deployed service by the co-researcher under existing work, combining a
multi-parameter air-quality module for particulates, carbon dioxide, temperature and humidity, a
discrete temperature and humidity sensor as a redundant reference, and a set of metal oxide gas
sensors. The expenditure therefore replicates a proven build rather than carrying development
risk. Nodes transmit to a cloud database over campus Wi-Fi or LoRa, with a public dashboard and a
display unit at a high-traffic campus location. Nodes are co-located with a rented
reference-grade instrument for a calibration campaign under Sibu conditions before deployment.

Two measurement limitations are stated here rather than left to the discussion. First, the
Malaysian Air Pollution Index is a Department of Environment index that incorporates a sulphur
dioxide sub-index, which this instrumentation does not measure. Sulphur dioxide bears on no
mechanism this project tests, so it is neither instrumented nor budgeted. The values reported are
accordingly an indicative campus air-quality index computed from the available sub-indices, and
are compared against the nearest Department of Environment monitoring station for the regional
signal rather than presented as an official index value. Second, low-cost optical particulate
sensors over-report mass at high relative humidity, because hygroscopic particle growth increases
light scattering, and this campus sits routinely in that regime. A published hygroscopic growth
correction is applied, both corrected and uncorrected values are reported, and relative humidity
is retained as a covariate in every model involving particulate matter. Any association between
particulate matter and fungal abundance, as in H5, is reported as a partial association after
that adjustment and is treated as exploratory.

Sampling, culture and contamination control protocols are agreed with SRDC-TROPI and validated
on a pilot round before the first formal round.

### Phase 2. Sampling and plating, April 2027 to July 2028

At each point a sterile template of ten centimetres by ten centimetres defines the sampled area.
Three replicate sterile swabs are taken per point per round, moistened in sterile transport
medium, with swab type held constant throughout, recognising that recovery efficiency is
swab-dependent [36]. Passive settle plates are deployed adjacent to a defined subset of points
for airborne spore capture.

Covariates are recorded at every point in every round. These are algal and cyanobacterial cover,
scored as percentage cover on a fixed ordinal scale; surface moisture presence; substrate
material; orientation; a shading index; and the preceding rainfall, temperature, humidity and
particulate record from the monitoring network.

Six quarterly sampling rounds are conducted between April 2027 and July 2028, with up to two
additional rounds triggered opportunistically during regional haze episodes. Round 1 falls in
April 2027 at the inter-monsoon period and the onset of the Southwest monsoon; Round 2 in July
2027 during the Southwest monsoon; Round 3 in October 2027 at the inter-monsoon period preceding
the Northeast monsoon; Round 4 in January 2028 during the Northeast monsoon; Round 5 in April
2028, a twelve-month repeat of Round 1; and Round 6 in July 2028, a twelve-month repeat of Round
2. Rounds 1 to 4 constitute a complete annual seasonal round covering both monsoon phases and
both inter-monsoon periods, while Rounds 5 and 6 provide paired year-on-year observations for two
of the four phases. The schedule is supplied as an attachment.

This is the maximum seasonal structure obtainable within a fixed twenty-four month award. The
project must close on 28 February 2029 without extension, so sampling ends in July 2028 in order
to leave seven months for the remaining molecular work, the analysis, both manuscripts and grant
closure. Full inter-annual replication across both monsoon phases lies outside the scope of this
award and is identified as follow-on work.

With thirty-six points per round, the three replicate swabs are composited per point per round,
giving thirty-six samples per round and two hundred and sixteen composited samples across the six
rounds.

Under the agreed division of labour, SRDC-TROPI supplies prepared sterile swabs and media to UTS
ahead of each round. UTS performs the swabbing and inoculates Sabouraud dextrose agar and potato
dextrose agar plates on site, then returns the inoculated plates and swabs to SRDC-TROPI under
cold-chain conditions for incubation. This keeps media preparation and incubation within the
partner's controlled laboratory environment while field sampling remains with the UTS team, and
it avoids duplicate procurement of the same consumables across the two budgets.

### Contamination control

Low-biomass surface sampling is vulnerable to contamination from reagents, kits and the air, so
the following controls are carried through the identical pipeline.

A field blank is taken once per round: a sterile swab is opened at the sampling site, exposed to
air for the duration of a normal swab, contacts no surface, and is processed identically. A
transport blank, being a sterile unopened swab, travels in the cooler to SRDC-TROPI and back once
per shipment. An extraction blank is run once per extraction batch, with the kit run on buffers
only and no sample input, capturing kit and reagent background; the batch membership of every
sample is recorded. A no-template control is run on each PCR plate, with water in place of
template, together with a positive control of a known isolate, so that a negative blank can be
distinguished from a failed reaction. On the culture side, uninoculated media plates from every
batch are incubated to confirm sterility, and an open plate is exposed during sample handling to
quantify laboratory fallout.

Three further rules apply. Blanks are carried through the entire pipeline and sequenced alongside
samples rather than screened out at the gel stage, since gel electrophoresis does not resolve
low-level contamination. The content of all blanks is reported in the results. Where sequence
data permits, contaminant signal is removed statistically by prevalence-based decontamination
against the blank set rather than by inspection.

### Phase 3. Incubation, isolation and identification, rolling, April 2027 to October 2028

Inoculated plates and swabs are transported to SRDC-TROPI in Kota Samarahan in sealed,
triple-packaged containers under cold-chain conditions. SRDC-TROPI incubates all plates and
enumerates growth, with colony counts expressed as colony-forming units per unit area for
surfaces and per cubic metre for settle plates. This quantitative dataset across all two hundred
and sixteen composited samples is the statistical backbone of H1 to H4.

Colonies are separated by morphotype and purified, and a curated set of up to sixty unique
isolates spanning the morphotype range across buildings, materials and seasons is selected for
identification. SRDC-TROPI performs DNA extraction and PCR amplification of the internal
transcribed spacer region using fungal-specific primers, and forwards the amplicons to an
accredited commercial provider for Sanger sequencing, funded from this Matching Grant under VOT
29000. Each isolate receives a single-pass read, with bidirectional reads on the subset whose
assignment is ambiguous, within the eighty sequencing reactions budgeted. Sequences are
quality-trimmed, assembled and assigned taxonomically against a curated fungal ITS reference
database, with the assignment method and confidence threshold stated, recognising that ITS
amplicon classification is method-dependent [40]. Isolates are maintained by SRDC-TROPI as a
referenced collection.

Processing is rolling rather than batched. Each sampling round is transported and processed as it
completes, so that first-year samples are identified while second-year sampling continues. This
yields an interim community profile at twelve months, permits protocol correction before two
years of samples have accumulated, and reduces schedule risk.

Two limitations of the identification approach are acknowledged in the design rather than the
discussion. First, this is a culture-dependent characterisation. Culture recovers viable
organisms but systematically underestimates non-culturable and stressed environmental taxa [35],
so the inventory describes the culturable fraction and is reported as such. Culture-independent
ITS amplicon profiling is identified as the priority extension. Second, the internal transcribed
spacer region is the accepted primary fungal barcode [39] but does not reliably resolve all
genera to species level. Assignments are therefore reported at genus level, with species-level
identification given only where ITS support is unambiguous. Taxa are described as putatively
toxigenic or biodeteriogenic on the basis of published activity for the genus [25, 26, 27, 18],
not on the basis of assay within this project. Secondary protein-coding markers for finer
resolution are identified as follow-on work.

### Phase 4. Analysis, synthesis and closure, August 2028 to February 2029

Colony-forming unit abundance is modelled using generalised linear mixed models appropriate to
count data, with building, orientation, facade material, design approach, season and algal cover
as fixed effects and sampling point as a random effect, so that repeated measures on the same
point are respected. Community composition is analysed by permutational multivariate analysis of
variance on Bray-Curtis dissimilarities, with ordination for visualisation, and alpha diversity
by standard indices. Paired colonised and clean surfaces are compared within point pairs. The
exploratory particulate association, H5, is fitted using antecedent-window particulate means with
humidity, rainfall and temperature as adjustment covariates, is reported as exploratory, and the
multiplicity of comparisons is acknowledged.

The findings are then translated into exterior material selection and maintenance guidance,
specifying which materials and orientations warrant intervention and at what interval, and into a
campus air-quality reporting protocol supporting institutional environmental reporting.

### Regulatory compliance, biosafety and data

Research conducted in Sarawak requires a permit issued by the Sarawak Biodiversity Council
through the Sarawak Online Research Application System. UTS holds SBC Permit No.
SBC-2026-RDP-10-AIO, issued on 28 July 2026 and valid until 27 July 2027. A variation covering
the project title, scope, personnel and institutions, together with a renewal, is submitted
through SORAS during the preparatory period before the project commences, so that sampling is not
gated by permit processing after the start date. Permit status, including the period granted on
renewal, is tracked as a named milestone with the Principal Researcher as owner. Deposition of
sequence data and of isolates is undertaken in accordance with the permit conditions.

Culturing concentrates fungal genera that include potentially pathogenic and toxigenic species.
Work is conducted at the containment level specified by the UTS biosafety assessment, with a
documented risk assessment, defined personal protective equipment, handling of cultures in a
biological safety cabinet, autoclave disposal of waste, and clearance for undergraduate and
postgraduate participation. Transport of biological material between institutions follows the
triple packaging and cold-chain requirements of the Agreement.

Raw air-quality records, colony counts, covariate records and sequence data are shared between
the parties for joint publication and reporting. Sequences are deposited in a public nucleotide
database with accessions reported, subject to permit conditions, and the air-quality dataset is
released as an open campus dataset.

The design is observational and establishes association rather than causation. It characterises
the culturable fraction of the community. Seasonal structure is assessed over one complete annual
round with two phases resampled twelve months apart, so year-on-year consistency can be tested
for those two phases only; full inter-annual replication across all four phases is not achievable
within a fixed twenty-four month term and is identified as follow-on work. Sampling is confined
to one campus, so generalisation to other Sarawak building stock is indicative, and the
stratified building selection is intended to maximise the transferable contrast available within
that constraint.

## Appendix A. Budget by vot

Total requested: RM25,000.00 over twenty-four months. Year 1 runs from March 2027 to February
2028 and Year 2 from March 2028 to February 2029. The text under each heading is written for
direct entry into the corresponding URCMS field. All supplier unit costs are planning figures
pending current quotations.

### VOT 11000, Salary and Wages, RM3,000.00

An undergraduate research assistant is engaged directly on field sampling, plate inoculation and
monitoring node assembly. The rate is RM7.00 per hour for a maximum of four hours per day, giving
RM28.00 per day. At approximately eighteen working days this gives RM500.00 per month, and
engagement runs for three active months per year, timed to the sampling rounds and the device
build. Year 1 is RM500.00 by three months, giving RM1,500.00, and Year 2 is the same, giving
RM1,500.00. The total is RM3,000.00, which is the stated maximum for temporary and contract
personnel.

Each of the six quarterly sampling rounds requires swabbing thirty-six fixed points across four
buildings and four facade orientations, on-site inoculation of agar plates, recording of
covariates and algal cover, and cold-chain packing for transfer to SRDC-TROPI, amounting to
approximately two full field days plus one preparation and packing day per round. The assistant
additionally supports assembly, enclosure fitting and field installation of the monitoring nodes
in Year 1. Engagement is concentrated into the active months rather than spread across the year,
so the allocation purchases approximately one hundred and seven assistant-days of direct project
labour. The appointment also delivers the human capital development outcome stated in Section 12.

### VOT 21000, Travelling and Transportation, RM3,000.00

Cold-chain transport of biological materials between Sibu and Kota Samarahan accounts for
RM1,350.00. SRDC-TROPI supplies prepared sterile swabs and media to UTS, and UTS returns
inoculated plates and swabs for incubation. Media are batched to cover two rounds per inbound
consignment, while returns must follow each round individually because the cultures are live.
Three inbound consignments and six return consignments give nine legs at RM150.00 each. The
materials are temperature-sensitive live cultures requiring sealed triple packaging under
cold-chain conditions, so ordinary post is not usable.

Delivery of purchased research materials accounts for RM150.00, covering inbound delivery of
sensor modules, enclosures and field consumables to UTS Sibu at RM50.00 per delivery for three
deliveries.

Conference presentation in Year 2 accounts for RM1,500.00, comprising return airfare at RM700.00,
accommodation at RM250.00 per night for three nights giving RM750.00, and ground transport to and
from airports at RM50.00. Subsistence is claimed only for paper presentation, as the guideline
permits. The conference registration fee is claimed separately under VOT 29000, which is where
the guideline places registration fees. Splitting the cost across the two vots is what allows a
realistic total conference provision of RM2,000.00 while keeping each vot within its ceiling.

Year 1 totals RM1,150.00, being RM900.00 of cold chain across two inbound and four return
consignments plus RM100.00 of deliveries. Year 2 totals RM1,850.00, being RM450.00 of cold chain
across one inbound and two return consignments, RM50.00 of delivery and RM1,500.00 of conference
travel. The total is RM3,000.00, which is the stated maximum for the Science, Engineering and
Technology area.

### VOT 24000, Rental, RM1,200.00

Rental of a reference-grade air quality analyser for co-location calibration of the low-cost
monitoring nodes under Sibu field conditions, at RM600.00 per week for two weeks. The first
campaign, in Year 1, calibrates all nodes before deployment. The second, also in Year 1, verifies
performance after field mounting.

Low-cost optical particulate sensors are subject to hygroscopic bias at the high relative
humidity prevailing on this campus, and metal oxide gas sensors drift. Co-located reference
measurement is what allows a correction to be derived and the reported values to be defensible
rather than merely indicative. Rental rather than purchase is appropriate because the instrument
is required for two short campaigns and not continuously. The item is placed in VOT 24000 rather
than VOT 35000 in line with the guideline treatment of rental.

### VOT 26000, Research Materials and Supplies, RM11,800.00

Air-quality monitoring node development accounts for RM8,040.00, covering components for the
fabrication of four campus monitoring nodes together with spares, in a configuration already
proven in deployment by the co-researcher under existing work, which removes design risk from
this project. The components are four multi-parameter air quality modules measuring carbon
dioxide, PM2.5, PM10, temperature and humidity at RM650.00 each, giving RM2,600.00; eight
discrete temperature and humidity sensors providing a redundant reference per node at RM25.00
each, giving RM200.00; four metal oxide gas sensor sets covering carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide,
ozone, ammonia and oxides of nitrogen at RM280.00 per set, giving RM1,120.00; six microcontroller
boards with breakout at RM90.00 each, giving RM540.00; one LoRa gateway and transmitter module
set at RM900.00; four ventilated weather-resistant enclosures rated to IP65 with mounting hardware
at RM220.00 each, giving RM880.00; four sets of power supply, solar panel, battery, cabling and
connectors at RM300.00 per set, giving RM1,200.00; and printed circuit board fabrication,
prototyping and soldering consumables at RM600.00.

Field sampling consumables account for RM2,560.00, comprising ten stainless sterile sampling
templates of ten centimetres by ten centimetres at RM60.00 each, giving RM600.00; four cold-chain
transport coolers with gel packs at RM180.00 each, giving RM720.00; personal protective equipment
comprising gloves, masks and disposable coveralls for six rounds and three personnel at RM700.00;
and ethanol, sample bags, labels, markers and field consumables at RM540.00.

A culture consumable contingency accounts for RM1,200.00, covering Sabouraud dextrose agar and
potato dextrose agar media, sterile swabs and Petri dishes. SRDC-TROPI supplies these items from
its matched research expenditure, so this allocation is a contingency only, covering the control
plates that the contamination control protocol requires, repeat sampling after a failed batch,
and any delay in partner supply. It ensures that the sampling programme is not interrupted and
does not duplicate the partner's provision.

Year 1 totals RM9,300.00 for node development and initial consumables, and Year 2 totals
RM2,500.00 for consumables, spares and node maintenance parts.

The node development is the largest single component because the project commits to continuous
campus air-quality and microclimate measurement as environmental context for the surface
sampling, and to a public dashboard and display. Fabricating nodes rather than purchasing
commercial analysers is what makes continuous multi-point monitoring affordable at this scale,
and because the configuration is already validated in service the expenditure replicates a
working design rather than funding development risk. Sulphur dioxide sensing is deliberately not
included, since it bears on no mechanism this project tests.

### VOT 29000, Professional Services, RM3,000.00

Sanger sequencing of ITS amplicons accounts for RM2,000.00, being eighty reactions at RM25.00 per
reaction. This is a quoted rate inclusive of sample quality control by gel electrophoresis and one
free re-run where a result is unsatisfactory. SRDC-TROPI performs DNA extraction and PCR
amplification of the internal transcribed spacer region from the curated isolate set, and the
amplicons are forwarded to the provider for sequencing. The eighty reactions cover a curated set
of up to sixty isolates spanning the morphotype range across four buildings, three facade
materials and six sampling rounds, by single-pass read, with bidirectional reads on the subset
whose assignment is ambiguous.

SORAS application and SBC permit fees account for RM500.00, covering the Sarawak Online Research
Application System submission and the Sarawak Biodiversity Council permit variation covering the
project title, scope, personnel and institutions, together with permit renewal. Research may not
be conducted in Sarawak without a valid permit, so this is a precondition of all fieldwork rather
than an optional cost.

The conference registration fee accounts for RM500.00, for one indexed conference in Year 2,
supporting the guideline requirement of two indexed conference or journal outputs. Associated
travel, accommodation and subsistence are claimed under VOT 21000, giving a total conference
provision of RM2,000.00 across the two vots.

Year 1 totals RM1,300.00, being RM800.00 of sequencing and RM500.00 of SORAS and permit fees.
Year 2 totals RM1,700.00, being RM1,200.00 of sequencing and RM500.00 of registration. The total
is RM3,000.00, which is the stated maximum for the Science, Engineering and Technology area.
Journal article processing charges are not requested, since the ceiling is committed to
sequencing, permits and conference registration, and journals without such charges are therefore
targeted for both indexed outputs.

### VOT 35000, Accessories and Equipment, RM3,000.00

An outdoor-rated display panel for public air-quality readings at RM2,200.00, together with
weatherproof display housing, mounting bracket and installation hardware at RM800.00. The total
is RM3,000.00, being twelve per cent of the grant and well within the forty per cent ceiling.

The public-facing display at a high-traffic campus location presents live readings and the
current campus air-quality classification, and is a deliverable of the collaboration agreement
between UTS and SRDC. It makes the monitoring visible to building occupants, which is the pathway
by which the project influences campus practice and supports institutional environmental
reporting. The item is procured following UTS Procurement Procedure, remains the property of UTS,
and is entered in the project asset inventory.
