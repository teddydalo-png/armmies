## 8. Executive summary

Building exteriors in equatorial Sarawak are continuously exposed to high humidity, heavy
rainfall and fluctuating air quality, including recurrent regional haze. Mould, algae and fungi
are among the most common organic contaminants on these surfaces, degrading both appearance and
material performance, yet exterior colonisation in the humid tropics is managed as a cleaning
problem rather than measured as a phenomenon. Which organisms colonise tropical educational
building facades, and how they vary with material, orientation, exposure and season, is largely
undocumented for equatorial Borneo.

This twenty-four month collaborative project between UTS and SRDC-TROPI characterises the fungal
and associated microbial communities colonising building exteriors at the UTS Sibu campus, and
records campus air quality and microclimate as environmental context. UTS will establish fixed
sampling points across four buildings stratified by facade material, age, design approach and
exposure, with paired visibly colonised and visually clean surfaces serving as internal
controls, and will sample quarterly across a full annual seasonal round with two phases
resampled twelve months apart. Plates inoculated at UTS are incubated, enumerated and isolated
at SRDC-TROPI, which performs DNA extraction and PCR; sequencing of selected isolates is
outsourced for identification against the internal transcribed spacer region. Algal and
cyanobacterial cover is scored at every point as a covariate, recognising these organisms as
pioneer colonisers that condition surfaces for fungal establishment.

Expected outputs are the first documented inventory of facade-colonising fungi for a tropical
Malaysian campus, two indexed joint publications, a curated isolate collection, exterior
material selection and maintenance guidance, a campus air-quality reporting protocol, and
trained student researchers. The project supports the Sarawak Post COVID-19 Development Strategy
2030 under Environmental Sustainability by providing MEITD, JKR and campus operators with
measured evidence on the biodeterioration of tropical public buildings (Economic Planning Unit Sarawak, 2021).

## 9. Background of research

### 9.1 Problem statement

Educational buildings in the humid tropics frequently develop visible biological colonisation on
exterior surfaces within a relatively short period after construction. At the University of
Technology Sarawak (UTS) campus in Sibu, which is a platinum-rated green building, mould growth,
algal films, and fungal staining are evident on multiple building facades despite modern design
approaches and routine maintenance. The prevailing management response is periodic cleaning and
chemical washing, which addresses aesthetic deterioration but does not identify the organisms
responsible, the environmental drivers of colonisation, or the underlying factors influencing
recurrence.

Three key knowledge gaps remain. First, the fungal communities colonising educational building
exteriors in tropical environments are poorly characterised. Although studies have documented
fungi in indoor educational environments and older buildings in Malaysia (Omar Hamdan et al.,
2026; Norback et al., 2016), comparable studies of exterior building surfaces are limited and are
largely derived from temperate or subtropical climates (Jain et al., 2009; Singh, 2000; Kosel et
al., 2024). Consequently, there is currently no published inventory of facade-associated fungi for
a tropical educational campus in Borneo.

Second, the environmental and architectural drivers of exterior fungal colonisation remain largely
unquantified. Moisture availability is consistently recognised as the principal determinant of
fungal growth on building materials (Dedesko and Siegel, 2015; Ito and Mizuno, 2009; Scott, 1994;
Shibuta and Teramoto, 2026). Factors such as facade orientation, shading, ventilation, material
composition, and rainfall exposure influence surface wetness and therefore microbial
establishment. However, these relationships have not been systematically investigated in tropical
educational buildings designed according to contemporary sustainability and green-building
principles. Third, maintenance and material-selection decisions are currently made without locally
derived evidence. Without understanding which fungal taxa are present, where they preferentially
colonise, and how environmental conditions influence their growth, facility managers are unable to
implement targeted prevention strategies or optimise maintenance schedules.

An important and unexplored question concerns the interaction between green building design and
fungal colonisation. Sustainable design strategies commonly employed in tropical environments,
including deep overhangs, extensive shading, natural ventilation, facade vegetation, and textured
exterior finishes, may unintentionally promote prolonged surface moisture retention. Because
persistent surface wetness is a key requirement for fungal establishment and growth (Ito and
Mizuno, 2009; Scott, 1994; Shibuta and Teramoto, 2026), it is possible that some green-building
features may increase susceptibility to biodeterioration under equatorial climatic conditions. To
the best of current knowledge, this relationship has not been examined in educational buildings in
Sarawak.

This project addresses these gaps through the molecular characterisation of fungal communities
colonising educational building exteriors and by evaluating the influence of facade
characteristics, microclimatic conditions, and campus air quality on fungal establishment and
persistence.

### 9.2 Hypotheses

H1. Fungal abundance and diversity differ significantly among facade orientations, with shaded and
low-insolation facades supporting greater fungal colonisation due to prolonged surface wetness (Ito
and Mizuno, 2009; Scott, 1994; Shibuta and Teramoto, 2026).

H2. Fungal community composition differs among facade materials, including exposed concrete,
painted render, and ceramic tile, reflecting differences in moisture retention and substrate
properties (Ara et al., 2025; Shibuta and Teramoto, 2026).

H3. Fungal abundance and community composition differ between Northeast and Southwest monsoon
periods, and these seasonal patterns remain consistent when corresponding periods are resampled
twelve months later (Liao et al., 2004; Medrela-Kuder, 2003).

H4. Surfaces exhibiting higher algal and cyanobacterial cover also exhibit greater fungal
abundance, reflecting facilitative interactions during biofilm establishment and surface
conditioning (Gaylarde and Morton, 1997).

H5. Fungal abundance is positively associated with antecedent particulate matter concentration
after accounting for relative humidity, rainfall, and temperature (Ramos, 2023).

### 9.3 Research questions

RQ1. Which fungal taxa colonise the exteriors of tropical educational buildings in Sibu, Sarawak,
and what are their relative abundances?

RQ2. How do fungal abundance, diversity, and community composition vary according to facade
material, building age, architectural design, and orientation?

RQ3. How does fungal community composition vary between seasonal monsoon phases, and are these
seasonal patterns consistent over time?

RQ4. Is algal and cyanobacterial cover associated with fungal abundance and community composition?

RQ5. Are fungal abundance and diversity associated with campus air-quality parameters and
microclimatic conditions after controlling for humidity and rainfall?

### 9.4 Literature review

Moisture availability is widely recognised as the primary determinant of fungal colonisation on
building materials. Relative humidity influences biodeterioration rates on mineral substrates (Jain
et al., 2009), material moisture adsorption characteristics affect fungal growth potential (Shibuta
and Teramoto, 2026), and hyphal extension rates increase under favourable humidity conditions (Ito
and Mizuno, 2009). Moisture-related variables have also been shown to structure fungal communities
on building surfaces (Dedesko and Siegel, 2015). Consequently, moisture persistence remains central
to understanding fungal establishment and biodeterioration processes (Scott, 1994).

Building design directly influences moisture persistence through its effects on ventilation,
shading, insolation, and rainfall exposure. Previous studies have focused on daylight optimisation,
energy efficiency, passive design strategies, and indoor environmental quality in educational
buildings (Alaidroos and Mosly, 2023; Idowu et al., 2026; Lim et al., 2026; Purnama et al., 2025),
and on the influence of occupant control of windows, doors and fans in naturally ventilated
classrooms (Franceschini et al., 2024; Lotun et al., 2026). However, biodeterioration risk is
rarely considered within these design frameworks despite its relevance to long-term building
performance and maintenance.

Studies of the built environment consistently report fungal genera such as Penicillium,
Cladosporium, and Aspergillus as dominant colonisers of indoor and outdoor surfaces (Ejdys and
Biedunkiewicz, 2011; Segers et al., 2015; Tanney et al., 2017), while emerging tropical and
subtropical taxa continue to be described (Wang et al., 2026). Malaysian studies have linked fungal
contamination in educational buildings with occupant health concerns and indoor environmental
quality (Omar Hamdan et al., 2026; Norback et al., 2016). Nevertheless, exterior fungal communities
of educational buildings in tropical Borneo remain undocumented.

The detrimental effects of fungal colonisation on construction materials are well established.
Fungi contribute to aesthetic deterioration, biofilm formation, mineral dissolution, physical
degradation, and material weakening through complex physiological and biochemical mechanisms (Jiang
et al., 2022). Deterioration has been demonstrated in concrete, marble, and other construction
materials under both laboratory and field conditions (Chaudhuri et al., 2020; Fomina et al., 2007;
Fu et al., 2025; Kosel et al., 2024). These processes are often enhanced by biofilm development and
extracellular polymeric substances that facilitate microbial persistence and substrate alteration
(Gaylarde and Morton, 1997).

Air quality may also influence fungal colonisation dynamics. Atmospheric particulate matter has
been associated with fungal spore concentrations in urban environments (Ramos, 2023), regional
spore concentrations can be modelled from atmospheric data (Vida et al., 2024), and integrated
particulate sensing is increasingly applied to bioaerosol dynamics (Barbosa et al., 2026).
Occupancy elevates measured fungal and bacterial loads in classrooms (Hospodsky et al., 2015),
while seasonal fluctuations strongly influence airborne fungal communities (Ebner et al., 1992;
Liao et al., 2004; Medrela-Kuder, 2003; Salonen et al., 2015a, 2015b). Monitoring microclimatic and
air-quality variables therefore provides important environmental context for interpreting patterns
of facade colonisation.

Molecular approaches have significantly improved the accuracy of fungal identification.
Culture-based methods recover viable fungi but may underestimate stressed or unculturable taxa,
whereas molecular techniques provide greater sensitivity and taxonomic resolution (Warren et al.,
2026), including multiplex real-time assays for filamentous fungi (Wei et al., 2024) and orthogonal
signals from fungal volatile organic compounds (Pantoja et al., 2016). The Internal Transcribed
Spacer (ITS) region remains the accepted primary fungal DNA barcode (Xu, 2016), making it
particularly suitable for characterising fungal communities associated with tropical building
exteriors, although taxonomic assignment of ITS amplicons depends on the classification method
adopted (Porter and Golding, 2011). Recovery efficiency is also influenced by the sampling device
used (Goverde et al., 2017).

Where intervention is required, the available options include antifungal nanocoatings based on
silver and zinc oxide (Abdel-Rahim et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2018) and non-thermal plasma
treatment (Roberts et al., 2025). An inventory of the taxa actually present on local facades is
what would allow such options to be evaluated for this building stock.

Despite advances in fungal ecology, biodeterioration science, and molecular detection, there
remains no integrated study examining fungal community composition, facade characteristics, air
quality, and microclimatic conditions on educational building exteriors in tropical Borneo. This
project addresses that gap and provides evidence to support building maintenance, material
selection, and sustainable design practices for public infrastructure in Sarawak.

## 11. Methodology

### Phase 1. Site stratification and setup, March to May 2027

Four buildings on the UTS Sibu campus are selected to generate contrast rather than to describe
a single structure. The stratification axes are facade material, covering exposed concrete,
painted render and tile; building age, contrasting newer and older blocks that differ in
cumulative exposure; design approach, contrasting green design features such as deep shading,
planting close to the envelope and natural ventilation against conventional construction; and
exposure, contrasting sheltered elevations against wind-exposed and rain-exposed ones. Facade
material inventory, orientation and shading geometry are mapped for each building.

At each building, fixed points are established on north, south, east and west facing walls
together with one roof-adjacent surface. At each wall point a pair of locations is established,
one visibly colonised and one visually clean, on the same material, to serve as an internal
control. This yields thirty-two wall points, being four buildings by four orientations by two
surface conditions, together with four roof points, giving thirty-six fixed points in total.
All points are georeferenced and photographed to permit repeat sampling.

Portable monitoring nodes are fabricated on a microcontroller platform in ventilated,
weather-resistant enclosures rated to IP65, measuring PM2.5, PM10, carbon monoxide, oxides of
nitrogen, ozone, carbon dioxide, temperature and relative humidity. The configuration follows one
already validated in deployed service by the co-researcher under existing work, combining a
multi-parameter air-quality module for particulates, carbon dioxide, temperature and humidity, a
discrete temperature and humidity sensor as a redundant reference, and a set of metal oxide gas
sensors. The expenditure therefore replicates a proven build rather than carrying development
risk. Nodes transmit to a cloud database over campus Wi-Fi or LoRa, with a public dashboard and a
display unit at a high-traffic campus location. Nodes are co-located with a rented
reference-grade instrument for a calibration campaign under Sibu conditions before deployment.

Two measurement limitations are stated here rather than left to the discussion. First, the
Malaysian Air Pollution Index is a Department of Environment index that incorporates a sulphur
dioxide sub-index, which this instrumentation does not measure. Sulphur dioxide bears on no
mechanism this project tests, so it is neither instrumented nor budgeted. The values reported are
accordingly an indicative campus air-quality index computed from the available sub-indices, and
are compared against the nearest Department of Environment monitoring station for the regional
signal rather than presented as an official index value. Second, low-cost optical particulate
sensors over-report mass at high relative humidity, because hygroscopic particle growth increases
light scattering, and this campus sits routinely in that regime. A published hygroscopic growth
correction is applied, both corrected and uncorrected values are reported, and relative humidity
is retained as a covariate in every model involving particulate matter. Any association between
particulate matter and fungal abundance, as in H5, is reported as a partial association after
that adjustment and is treated as exploratory.

Sampling, culture and contamination control protocols are agreed with SRDC-TROPI and validated
on a pilot round before the first formal round.

### Phase 2. Sampling and plating, June 2027 to September 2028

At each point a sterile template of ten centimetres by ten centimetres defines the sampled area.
Three replicate sterile swabs are taken per point per round, moistened in sterile transport
medium, with swab type held constant throughout, recognising that recovery efficiency is
swab-dependent (Goverde et al., 2017). Passive settle plates are deployed adjacent to a defined subset of points
for airborne spore capture.

Covariates are recorded at every point in every round. These are algal and cyanobacterial cover,
scored as percentage cover on a fixed ordinal scale; surface moisture presence; substrate
material; orientation; a shading index; and the preceding rainfall, temperature, humidity and
particulate record from the monitoring network.

Six quarterly sampling rounds are conducted between June 2027 and September 2028, with up to
two additional rounds triggered opportunistically during regional haze episodes. Round 1 falls in
June 2027 during the Southwest monsoon; Round 2 in September 2027 in the late Southwest phase;
Round 3 in December 2027 during the Northeast monsoon; Round 4 in March 2028 in the late Northeast
phase; Round 5 in June 2028, a twelve-month repeat of Round 1; and Round 6 in September 2028, a
twelve-month repeat of Round 2. Rounds 1 to 4 constitute a complete annual seasonal round covering
both monsoon phases, while Rounds 5 and 6 provide paired year-on-year observations for two of
those phases. The schedule is supplied as an attachment.

This is the maximum seasonal structure obtainable within a fixed twenty-four month award that also
allows for permit processing before sampling begins. The project must close on 28 February 2029
without extension, so sampling ends in September 2028 in order to leave five months for the
remaining molecular work, the analysis, both manuscripts and grant closure. Full inter-annual
replication across both monsoon phases lies outside the scope of this award and is identified as
follow-on work.

With thirty-six points per round, the three replicate swabs are composited per point per round,
giving thirty-six samples per round and two hundred and sixteen composited samples across the six
rounds.

Under the agreed division of labour, SRDC-TROPI supplies prepared sterile swabs and media to UTS
ahead of each round. UTS performs the swabbing and inoculates Sabouraud dextrose agar and potato
dextrose agar plates on site, then returns the inoculated plates and swabs to SRDC-TROPI under
cold-chain conditions for incubation. This keeps media preparation and incubation within the
partner's controlled laboratory environment while field sampling remains with the UTS team, and
it avoids duplicate procurement of the same consumables across the two budgets.

### Contamination control

Low-biomass surface sampling is vulnerable to contamination from reagents, kits and the air, so
the following controls are carried through the identical pipeline.

A field blank is taken once per round: a sterile swab is opened at the sampling site, exposed to
air for the duration of a normal swab, contacts no surface, and is processed identically. A
transport blank, being a sterile unopened swab, travels in the cooler to SRDC-TROPI and back once
per shipment. An extraction blank is run once per extraction batch, with the kit run on buffers
only and no sample input, capturing kit and reagent background; the batch membership of every
sample is recorded. A no-template control is run on each PCR plate, with water in place of
template, together with a positive control of a known isolate, so that a negative blank can be
distinguished from a failed reaction. On the culture side, uninoculated media plates from every
batch are incubated to confirm sterility, and an open plate is exposed during sample handling to
quantify laboratory fallout.

Three further rules apply. Blanks are carried through the entire pipeline and sequenced alongside
samples rather than screened out at the gel stage, since gel electrophoresis does not resolve
low-level contamination. The content of all blanks is reported in the results. Where sequence
data permits, contaminant signal is removed statistically by prevalence-based decontamination
against the blank set rather than by inspection.

### Phase 3. Incubation, isolation and identification, rolling, June 2027 to December 2028

Inoculated plates and swabs are transported to SRDC-TROPI in Kota Samarahan in sealed,
triple-packaged containers under cold-chain conditions. SRDC-TROPI incubates all plates and
enumerates growth, with colony counts expressed as colony-forming units per unit area for
surfaces and per cubic metre for settle plates. This quantitative dataset across all two hundred
and sixteen composited samples is the statistical backbone of H1 to H4.

Colonies are separated by morphotype and purified, and a curated set of up to sixty unique
isolates spanning the morphotype range across buildings, materials and seasons is selected for
identification. SRDC-TROPI performs DNA extraction and PCR amplification of the internal
transcribed spacer region using fungal-specific primers, and forwards the amplicons to an
accredited commercial provider for Sanger sequencing, funded from this Matching Grant under VOT
29000. Each isolate receives a single-pass read, with bidirectional reads on the subset whose
assignment is ambiguous, within the eighty sequencing reactions budgeted. Sequences are
quality-trimmed, assembled and assigned taxonomically against a curated fungal ITS reference
database, with the assignment method and confidence threshold stated, recognising that ITS
amplicon classification is method-dependent (Porter and Golding, 2011). Isolates are maintained by SRDC-TROPI as a
referenced collection.

Processing is rolling rather than batched. Each sampling round is transported and processed as it
completes, so that first-year samples are identified while second-year sampling continues. This
yields an interim community profile at twelve months, permits protocol correction before two
years of samples have accumulated, and reduces schedule risk.

Two limitations of the identification approach are acknowledged in the design rather than the
discussion. First, this is a culture-dependent characterisation. Culture recovers viable
organisms but systematically underestimates non-culturable and stressed environmental taxa (Warren et al., 2026),
so the inventory describes the culturable fraction and is reported as such. Culture-independent
ITS amplicon profiling is identified as the priority extension. Second, the internal transcribed
spacer region is the accepted primary fungal barcode (Xu, 2016) but does not reliably resolve all
genera to species level. Assignments are therefore reported at genus level, with species-level
identification given only where ITS support is unambiguous. Taxa are described as putatively
toxigenic or biodeteriogenic on the basis of published activity for the genus (Jiang et al., 2022; Chaudhuri et al., 2020; Fomina et al., 2007; Gaylarde and Morton, 1997),
not on the basis of assay within this project. Secondary protein-coding markers for finer
resolution are identified as follow-on work.

### Phase 4. Analysis, synthesis and closure, October 2028 to February 2029

Colony-forming unit abundance is modelled using generalised linear mixed models appropriate to
count data, with building, orientation, facade material, design approach, season and algal cover
as fixed effects and sampling point as a random effect, so that repeated measures on the same
point are respected. Community composition is analysed by permutational multivariate analysis of
variance on Bray-Curtis dissimilarities, with ordination for visualisation, and alpha diversity
by standard indices. Paired colonised and clean surfaces are compared within point pairs. The
exploratory particulate association, H5, is fitted using antecedent-window particulate means with
humidity, rainfall and temperature as adjustment covariates, is reported as exploratory, and the
multiplicity of comparisons is acknowledged.

The findings are then translated into exterior material selection and maintenance guidance,
specifying which materials and orientations warrant intervention and at what interval, and into a
campus air-quality reporting protocol supporting institutional environmental reporting.

### Regulatory compliance, biosafety and data

Research conducted in Sarawak requires a permit issued by the Sarawak Biodiversity Council
through the Sarawak Online Research Application System. An application for a permit covering this
project, its scope, its personnel and the participating institutions is submitted through SORAS on
commencement of the award. No surface sampling, and no collection or transfer of biological
material, takes place until that permit is issued.

The sampling schedule is set to accommodate the permit processing period. The first round is
scheduled for June 2027, three months after commencement, and the preceding months are occupied by
work that requires no permit: node fabrication and calibration, establishment and georeferencing of
the sampling points, the facade material and shading inventory, and the visual survey of algal and
cyanobacterial cover. Should issuance nonetheless fall later than planned, the rounds commence at
the next quarterly point following issue and the closure date is unchanged. Permit status is
tracked as a named milestone with the Principal Researcher as owner, and deposition of sequence
data and of isolates is undertaken in accordance with the permit conditions.

Culturing concentrates fungal genera that include potentially pathogenic and toxigenic species.
Work is conducted at the containment level specified by the UTS biosafety assessment, with a
documented risk assessment, defined personal protective equipment, handling of cultures in a
biological safety cabinet, autoclave disposal of waste, and clearance for undergraduate and
postgraduate participation. Transport of biological material between institutions follows the
triple packaging and cold-chain requirements of the Agreement.

Raw air-quality records, colony counts, covariate records and sequence data are shared between
the parties for joint publication and reporting. Sequences are deposited in a public nucleotide
database with accessions reported, subject to permit conditions, and the air-quality dataset is
released as an open campus dataset.

The design is observational and establishes association rather than causation. It characterises
the culturable fraction of the community. Seasonal structure is assessed over one complete annual
round with two phases resampled twelve months apart, so year-on-year consistency can be tested
for those two phases only; full inter-annual replication across all four phases is not achievable
within a fixed twenty-four month term and is identified as follow-on work. Sampling is confined
to one campus, so generalisation to other Sarawak building stock is indicative, and the
stratified building selection is intended to maximise the transferable contrast available within
that constraint.

fraction of the community; functional degradation assay of the recovered isolates on cementitious
coupons; and full inter-annual replication across both monsoon phases. All three are made
possible by the isolate collection and baseline dataset this award produces, and none is
affordable or achievable within the present ceiling and term.
